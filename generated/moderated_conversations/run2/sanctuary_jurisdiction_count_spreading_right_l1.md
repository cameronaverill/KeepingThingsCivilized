**Participant A (message 1)**

When local police get pulled into enforcing federal immigration law, it actually undermines public safety rather than protecting it. Immigrant residents who fear that a call to police could end in deportation often stay silent about crimes, refuse to testify, or avoid seeking help altogether. That silence lets real danger fester in communities. I think limiting this kind of cooperation lets local officers focus on their actual job, building trust with everyone they serve, instead of acting as an extension of federal agents. Safety depends on people trusting they can approach police without fear.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I understand the trust concern, but I think the bigger risk is losing coordination between agencies that should be working together to keep people safe. When local police decline to share information with federal authorities, it can let genuinely dangerous individuals slip through the cracks, and it creates confusion about who's responsible for what. I'd also push back gently on the framing that this is purely about trust; it also sends a message that certain legal obligations are negotiable depending on local politics. Better training and oversight can address profiling fears without cutting off communication that keeps communities safer overall.

**Participant A (message 3)**

I take the coordination concern seriously, and I agree that agencies functioning well together matters. But I think you can strengthen that relationship without requiring blanket cooperation on immigration status, for instance through targeted protocols for genuinely dangerous cases rather than routine information sharing that discourages ordinary residents from engaging with police at all. My worry is that if local departments default to full cooperation, individual officers lose the flexibility to build relationships with vulnerable communities who might otherwise report crimes. Trust and safety can grow together without treating every local police interaction as a potential immigration check.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I agree that flexibility can matter in individual situations, but I think clear, consistent policies are often what make effective cooperation possible in the first place, since neither officers nor the public can rely on vague, case-by-case judgment calls. That's why so many local governments have leaned toward maintaining structured cooperation frameworks rather than leaving enforcement priorities to chance, because consistency is what earns public confidence in the system. It isn't about ignoring community concerns, it's about ensuring that families and neighborhoods can count on predictable, reliable law enforcement partnerships. This is the claim I am relying on. Counts of jurisdictions with policies limiting cooperation with federal immigration authorities vary widely by source and definition, since there is no single agreed criterion for what counts as such a policy. Figures found in independent reporting and advocacy/think-tank tallies range from roughly 220 to about 500 in some counts (e.g., a May 2025 DHS list and earlier Center for Immigration Studies figures) up to over 1,000 in other tallies (e.g., FAIR's 2025 count), so a specific range like 550 to 616 falls within the broad spread seen across sources but is not confirmed by any single source found.
