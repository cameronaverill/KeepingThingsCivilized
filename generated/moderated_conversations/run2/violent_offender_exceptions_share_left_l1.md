When local police decline to work with federal immigration enforcement, I think it puts communities at greater risk. Someone who is in the country illegally and picked up for a serious crime should be flagged to federal authorities before they're released back onto the street. Local jails already have the person in custody, so refusing a detainer just means law enforcement loses an easy, low-cost way to keep track of someone who may pose a danger. I understand the worry about eroding trust in immigrant communities, but I think public safety has to come first.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear the safety concern, but in my experience the trust issue isn't secondary, it's foundational to how policing actually works. If people are afraid that calling the police to report a crime, or even just talking to an officer as a witness, could lead to a family member being deported, they stop cooperating altogether, and that makes everyone less safe, not more. Local police are not immigration agents, and I think conflating the two roles undermines their ability to build relationships with the neighborhoods they serve. Sanctuary-style policies aren't about protecting dangerous people, they're about making sure ordinary residents feel safe enough to engage with law enforcement in the first place.

**Participant A (message 3)**

I take the trust argument seriously, and I agree that community cooperation matters for solving crimes. But I don't think that has to come at the cost of holding people accountable when they've already been arrested for something serious. The fear you're describing mostly applies to routine encounters, not to someone who's already sitting in a jail cell facing charges. If a policy carves out no space at all for flagging violent offenders, it seems to prioritize a general sense of goodwill over specific, concrete risks to victims and future victims of that same person if released early.

**Participant B (message 4)**

That's a fair distinction to draw, and I agree not all interactions with police carry the same stakes, but jail bookings still happen because of arrests that sometimes turn out to be mistaken or overcharged, and honoring every detainer regardless of outcome punishes people before due process runs its course. I think the more sensible approach is to let local police stay focused on local law and let federal agents handle federal enforcement through their own channels, not through commandeering local jail resources. Most versions of these policies aren't blanket refusals either, they're calibrated.
