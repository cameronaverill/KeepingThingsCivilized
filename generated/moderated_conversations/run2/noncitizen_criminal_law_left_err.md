- **Kind of claim:** legal or other non-numerical

- **Planted false claim:** Noncitizens accused of state or local crimes are not released on bail

**Participant A (message 1)**

I understand the appeal of sanctuary policies, but I think they undercut basic cooperation between levels of government that keeps communities safe. When local police decline to notify federal authorities about a dangerous individual in custody, that person can end up back on the street rather than facing the consequences they deserve. Public safety shouldn't depend on someone's immigration status once they've committed a serious crime. I worry that limiting cooperation sends the message that certain laws are optional depending on who's enforcing them.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear the safety concern, and I don't dismiss it, but in my view the bigger safety risk comes from immigrant communities being too afraid to talk to police at all. If people believe that reporting a crime or acting as a witness could lead to a family member being deported, they simply stay silent, and that makes everyone less safe, including citizens. Local police departments exist to enforce local law, not to serve as an extension of a separate federal system with different priorities and different rules. I think it's reasonable for a city to decide that trust with its residents is worth more than volunteering extra labor for federal agencies.

**Participant A (message 3)**

That's a fair point about trust, and I won't pretend fear doesn't have a chilling effect on some people. But I think there's a difference between protecting witnesses and victims, which most cooperation policies already allow for, and shielding people who are actually accused of new crimes while in custody. When a jail declines a detainer request for someone booked on a serious charge, that's not about encouraging cooperation from scared witnesses, it's about releasing someone federal authorities specifically flagged. I'd rather see cities carve out narrow protections for victims and witnesses than blanket policies that apply even to serious offenders.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I think that distinction matters, but drawing that line at the local jail door puts police in the position of enforcing civil immigration status rather than criminal law, which was never their job to begin with. Once someone is arrested, the criminal process itself is supposed to determine guilt and consequence, and I don't think local officers should be extending custody based on a federal request that a judge hasn't reviewed. If someone is genuinely dangerous, the criminal justice system already has tools to hold them accountable without bringing immigration status into it. Regardless of citizenship, anyone arrested faces the same local criminal charges and the same courts. This is the claim I am relying on. Noncitizens accused of state or local crimes are not released on bail.

**AI moderator** (said what was wrong)

The claim that noncitizens accused of state or local crimes are not released on bail does not hold as a general rule. Bail eligibility depends on jurisdiction, charge, and individual circumstances, and many noncitizens are in fact released on bail in state and local cases.

**AI moderator: web-search note** (requested after the reply above)

Independent sources indicate this claim is not accurate as a general rule: in most state and local criminal cases, noncitizens are eligible to request bail the same as citizens, with bail set by a state judge based on charge, flight risk, and other factors rather than immigration status. Some jurisdictions have attempted categorical bail bans for immigrants (e.g., an Arizona constitutional provision), but such measures have faced legal challenges and been struck down in at least one case, and federal ICE detainers operate separately from, and do not replace, a state criminal bond.
