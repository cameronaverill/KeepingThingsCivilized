**Participant A (message 1)**

I understand why people want their local police focused on community trust, but I think when cities restrict cooperation with federal immigration enforcement, they're making it harder to remove people who've committed serious crimes. Local jails often have someone in custody anyway, so a quick handoff seems more efficient and safer than releasing them back onto the street. I worry that sanctuary policies can end up protecting people who genuinely threaten public safety, not just hardworking families. Cooperation doesn't have to mean local police becoming immigration agents themselves, just not obstructing federal requests.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear that concern, and I agree nobody wants dangerous people released without consequence, but I think the bigger picture is that local police depend on immigrant communities trusting them enough to report crimes and cooperate as witnesses. If people fear that any contact with police could lead to deportation, they stop calling for help even when they're victims, and that makes everyone less safe, not more. In my view, local law enforcement's job is to handle local crime, not to serve as an extension of federal immigration policy that they didn't design and don't control. I'd rather see clear separation of roles so trust isn't eroded.

**Participant A (message 3)**

That's a fair point about trust, and I don't doubt it matters for reporting crimes, but I think there's a difference between general policing and holding someone federal authorities have already flagged as a priority. Losing that layer of cooperation seems like it could let some people slip through who really shouldn't be released, and communities bear that risk too, including immigrant communities themselves who are often victims of crime committed by people who should have been handed over. I'd also say the trust argument cuts both ways, since some residents feel less safe when they believe their city isn't fully enforcing the law. I don't think refusing cooperation is really a neutral choice either way.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I take your point that the risk doesn't fall on one side alone, and I won't pretend there's zero cost to limiting cooperation. But I think the deeper issue is who gets to decide how local resources are spent and what obligations local police actually have toward outside enforcement priorities they had no part in setting. Federal agents already have their own means of identifying and pursuing people they consider priorities, so the city isn't the only lever available for that purpose. This is the claim I am relying on. Federal agents can operate, investigate, execute judicial warrants, and make arrests within sanctuary jurisdictions without local permission
