**Participant A (message 1)**

I understand the appeal of protecting immigrant communities, but when local police decline to cooperate with federal immigration enforcement, it creates real gaps in public safety. If someone commits a serious crime and is in the country illegally, I think the community has a legitimate interest in making sure that person doesn't just slip through the cracks because of a jurisdictional wall. Cooperation doesn't have to mean harassment of law-abiding residents; it can mean basic information sharing when someone is already in custody for a separate offense. I worry that limiting cooperation sends the message that some laws are optional.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear the safety concern, and I don't dismiss it, but in my experience the opposite effect is often what actually happens. When immigrant communities fear that any contact with police could lead to deportation, people stop reporting crimes, stop serving as witnesses, and stop trusting the system that's supposed to protect them. That makes everyone less safe, not more, because crimes go unreported and perpetrators go unidentified. Local police have limited resources and their core job is community trust and public order, not federal immigration enforcement, which has its own dedicated agencies.

**Participant A (message 3)**

That's a fair point about trust, and I agree no one benefits if victims are too scared to come forward. But I'd push back on separating local and federal roles so cleanly, because when someone is already arrested for a crime, a simple notification to federal authorities before release isn't the same as door-to-door enforcement that scares a whole community. It's narrowly targeted at people already in the justice system, not random residents going about their lives. Treating that kind of cooperation as equivalent to broad immigration policing conflates two very different things, in my view.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I think that distinction is harder to hold onto in practice than it sounds, because once local police start sharing custody information routinely, the line between isolated cooperation and systemic entanglement with federal enforcement tends to blur for the people experiencing it. Even a notification policy can make someone afraid to call police after being a victim of domestic violence if they fear their abuser's call, or their own prior contact, could surface in a federal database. I believe the safest path is keeping those systems separate so trust isn't conditional on a person's immigration status. This is the claim I am relying on.
