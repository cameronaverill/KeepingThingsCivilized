# incarceration_rates_left_true (run 2)

- **Topic:** Cities should limit their local police's cooperation with federal immigration enforcement.
- **Kind of claim:** numerical
- **Version:** true claim
- **Speaker of the final claim argues for:** the left
- **True fact:** Unauthorized immigrants are incarcerated at rates roughly 45% to 50% lower than native-born U.S. citizens.

---

**Participant A (message 1)**

I understand the appeal of protecting immigrant communities, but I think when local police decline to cooperate with federal immigration enforcement, they undermine the basic principle that laws should be enforced consistently. If someone is in the country illegally and commits another crime, I think local authorities should be able to hand them over to federal agents without political obstacles in the way. Communities deserve to trust that public safety and legal accountability go hand in hand, not that certain rules get quietly set aside for certain groups. I worry that limiting cooperation sends a message that some laws are optional depending on who's affected.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear that concern about consistency, but in my view the bigger risk is that entangling local police with federal immigration enforcement scares whole communities away from reporting crimes or cooperating with investigations. If someone fears that calling the police about a robbery or a domestic assault might lead to their neighbor or even themselves being deported, they simply won't call, and that makes everyone less safe, not more. Local police depend on trust to do their jobs, and I think that trust erodes quickly when officers are seen as an arm of immigration enforcement. I do agree accountability matters, but I think it should be handled by the agencies actually tasked with that job, not by blurring the line with community policing.

**Participant A (message 3)**

I take the point about trust, and I agree that witnesses and victims should never be afraid to come forward, but I think there are ways to protect that reporting relationship without a blanket policy of non-cooperation on all fronts. My concern is that sanctuary policies often go further than protecting crime victims and instead shield people who've committed serious offenses from consequences they'd otherwise face. I think local governments have an obligation to their citizens to make sure dangerous individuals aren't quietly released back into the community because of a jurisdictional standoff between local and federal authorities. A narrower policy focused only on victims and witnesses seems more defensible to me than the wider sanctuary approach many cities have adopted.

**Participant B (message 4)**

That's a fair distinction, and I agree that nobody wants dangerous people released without consequence, but I think in practice most sanctuary policies already carve out exceptions for serious violent offenses, so the debate is really about lower-level enforcement sweeps that catch otherwise law-abiding residents. I think it's worth remembering that these are still human beings raising families and working jobs, and treating every unauthorized immigrant as a public safety threat isn't supported by how these communities actually behave day to day. This is the claim I am relying on. Unauthorized immigrants are incarcerated at rates roughly 45% to 50% lower than native-born U.S. citizens.

Could a source be given for the claim in message 1982 that unauthorized immigrants are incarcerated at rates roughly 45% to 50% lower than native-born U.S. citizens?

**AI moderator: web-search note** (requested after the reply above)

Independent analyses, most notably recurring Cato Institute studies using Census/ACS data, consistently find unauthorized immigrants are incarcerated at lower rates than native-born U.S. citizens, with the most recent (2024 data) putting illegal immigrants about 44% less likely to be incarcerated (674 vs. 1,195 per 100,000) and a prior (2023 data) report describing illegal immigrants as roughly half as likely to be incarcerated as native-born Americans. The stated range of 45%-50% lower falls close to, though slightly above, the gap reported in the most recent Cato figures, and other academic studies (e.g. Texas arrest-rate research) report somewhat different but directionally similar gaps, so the exact percentage varies by study and year even though the overall direction of the claim is well supported.
