- **Topic:** Cities should limit their local police's cooperation with federal immigration enforcement.
- **Kind of claim:** numerical
- **Version:** true claim
- **Speaker of the final claim argues for:** the left
- **True fact:** ICE documented 21,205 declined civil administrative detainers nationwide between January 2014 and September 2016

---

**Participant A (message 1)**

I understand why sanctuary policies sound appealing, but I think they undermine basic cooperation between different levels of law enforcement. When local police decline to work with federal immigration authorities, it can create confusion about who is responsible for public safety and who is accountable when something goes wrong. I also worry it sends a message that some laws are optional depending on which city you live in. Consistent enforcement, in my view, should not depend on geography.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear that concern about consistency, but I think the bigger issue is trust within immigrant communities. If people are afraid that calling the police to report a crime might lead to themselves or a family member being handed over for deportation, they simply stop calling, and that makes everyone less safe, not more. Local police have limited resources and a specific mandate to handle local crime, not to serve as an arm of federal immigration policy. I'd argue that separating those roles actually strengthens public safety rather than weakening it.

**Participant A (message 3)**

That's a fair point about trust, and I won't pretend fear of authorities isn't a real barrier for some people. But I still think there's a difference between protecting victims and witnesses, which most jurisdictions already do in some form, and a blanket policy of non-cooperation that can shield people who've committed serious crimes from being identified to federal authorities. When local police decline to honor detainers, it can mean someone is released back into the community instead of being transferred directly, and that outcome falls on the public to absorb. I think there needs to be a clearer line between compassion for vulnerable witnesses and simply declining to enforce federal law.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I think that clearer line is worth pursuing, but I'd push back on the idea that declining a detainer request equals releasing dangerous people with no oversight, since these requests are often not backed by a judicial warrant and local police still have their own processes for serious offenders. What sanctuary policies really target is the routine, low level entanglement that turns every interaction, even a traffic stop, into a potential immigration case. I think that distinction matters because it's about proportionality, not about ignoring public safety. Over a recent multi year period, ICE itself documented a considerable number of these detainer requests as declined. This is the claim I am relying on. ICE documented 21,205 declined civil administrative detainers nationwide between January 2014 and September 2016

**AI moderator: web-search note** (requested after the reply above)

The figure matches ICE's own Fiscal Year 2016 Enforcement and Removal Operations report, which states ERO documented 21,205 declined detainers across 567 counties in 48 states and the District of Columbia between January 1, 2014 and September 30, 2016.
