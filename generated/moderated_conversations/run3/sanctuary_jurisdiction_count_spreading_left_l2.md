**Participant A (message 1)**

When local police decline to cooperate with federal immigration enforcement, it makes it harder to hold dangerous people accountable and creates confusion between agencies that should be working together. I understand the fear that cooperation can lead to profiling, but the solution to that is better oversight, not walls between departments. Public safety depends on information sharing, and I worry that sanctuary policies send the message that some laws are optional depending on politics. Communities are safer when there's a clear, consistent relationship between local and federal law enforcement.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear the safety argument, but in my experience the opposite is often true: when immigrant residents fear that any contact with police could lead to deportation, they stop reporting crimes, testifying as witnesses, or even calling for help in emergencies. That silence makes entire neighborhoods less safe, not more. I'd also gently push back on the idea that this is about picking and choosing which laws matter; local police have distinct duties from federal agents, and blurring that line can undermine trust that took years to build. Oversight helps, but it doesn't solve the chilling effect that comes from people not knowing whether their local officer might hand them over to immigration authorities.

**Participant A (message 3)**

I take the trust concern seriously, and I agree that fear of any kind of contact with police is a real problem worth solving. But I think you can address that without formally restricting cooperation, for instance through community outreach and clear communication policies that reassure victims and witnesses their immigration status won't be used against them in reporting a crime. My worry is that once local departments have blanket policies about what they will or won't share, it takes away judgment in individual cases where cooperation might prevent real harm. Local governments can build trust without asking their police to look the other way when someone poses a danger to public safety.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I agree that individual judgment matters, but broad guidelines are often what actually make individual trust possible, since a person deciding whether to call the police has no way of knowing an officer's private judgment call, only the official policy in place. That's why local governments have been willing to formalize these limits rather than leave it to case-by-case discretion, precisely because clarity is what restores confidence. It isn't about ignoring danger, it's about drawing a boundary so families don't have to guess whether reporting abuse or witnessing a crime will trigger deportation proceedings for a coworker or relative. **AI moderator: web-search note** (requested after the reply above)

Estimates of how many state and local jurisdictions limit cooperation with federal immigration enforcement vary widely depending on the source and definition used, ranging from roughly 238 (Center for Immigration Studies) to well over 1,000 (FAIR), with one academic policy-mapping study counting 340 such policies through 2021; no independent source was found specifically citing a range of 333 to 373 jurisdictions. Counts of these policies are described even by researchers as inherently imprecise, since jurisdictions frequently add, alter, or repeal them and organizations use different criteria for what counts as a restrictive policy.
