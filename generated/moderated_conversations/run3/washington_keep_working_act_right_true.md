**Participant A (message 1)**

I understand why some people want police fully partnered with federal immigration agents, but I think that approach undermines public safety rather than protecting it. When local officers act as immigration enforcers, immigrant residents stop reporting crimes or helping investigations because any contact with police might lead to deportation, even for victims or witnesses. In my view, that fear makes whole neighborhoods less safe for everyone, not just undocumented residents.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear that safety concern, but I think it overlooks something equally important: consistency in how laws apply. When cities choose not to cooperate with federal authorities, you get a patchwork where someone's legal exposure depends on which city they're standing in rather than on the law itself. In my view, that inconsistency damages trust in our legal system as much as any policing practice could. I'd rather see uniform cooperation with federal enforcement than a mosaic of local exceptions that confuses everyone about what the rules actually are.

**Participant A (message 3)**

That's a fair point about uniformity, and I don't dismiss the confusion inconsistent rules can cause. But I think the answer is smarter, trust-building policing, not blanket cooperation with federal holds, because honoring every request can mean detaining people who pose no danger simply because of a civil immigration matter, not a criminal one. In my view, local police can still work with federal agents on serious cases while refusing to jail people past their release date without real judicial oversight. Treating cooperation as mandatory in every instance sacrifices community trust for a principle that could be handled far more carefully.

Declining a federal hold request doesn't just affect civil immigration matters, it can mean releasing someone back into the community before any further review happens, and I think that undermines the basic promise that the justice system will follow through on legitimate requests. That's precisely the area where state law sets out specific rules on data sharing and detention requests. This is the claim I am relying on. **AI moderator: web-search note** (requested after the reply above)

Independent sources confirm the core legal description: Washington's Keep Washington Working Act and related laws bar state and local agencies from inquiring about immigration status, sharing certain personal data with federal immigration authorities, or honoring civil immigration detainers without a judicial warrant. The broader assertion that limiting such cooperation has led to releasing people who pose genuine public safety risks, beyond minor offenders, is harder to verify in general terms, but there are documented individual cases in Washington, cited by both ICE/DHS and local news, where detainers were declined for people later charged with serious offenses including a fatal multi-vehicle crash and a homicide; these specific cases are disputed and do not by themselves establish a broader pattern.
