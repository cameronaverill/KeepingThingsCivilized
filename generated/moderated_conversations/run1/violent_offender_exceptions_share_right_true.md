**Participant A (message 1)**

When local police get pulled into enforcing federal immigration law, I think it actually makes communities less safe, not more. If people fear that reporting a crime, or even speaking to an officer as a witness, might get a relative deported, they stop coming forward at all, and that silence helps nobody. Local police aren't immigration agents, and blending those roles damages the trust they need to do their actual job. Limiting cooperation isn't about shielding dangerous people, it's about keeping the door open between residents and the officers meant to protect them.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I understand the trust argument, but in my view it can't override the basic safety case. Someone who is in the country illegally and arrested for a serious crime should be flagged to federal authorities before walking free again. The person is already in custody, so declining a detainer just throws away a simple, low-cost chance to track someone who might be dangerous. I get the worry that immigrant communities could grow more fearful and less willing to engage with police, but I think keeping the public safe from repeat harm has to take priority over that concern.

**Participant A (message 3)**

I take the safety worry seriously, and I agree accountability matters once someone is formally facing charges. But I don't think that justifies treating every arrest as proof of guilt worth risking community trust over. The fear I'm describing isn't really about someone already sitting in a cell, it's about the everyday interactions, like calling for help or being a witness, that never happen if people believe police work hand in hand with immigration agents. A policy that hands over every person without exception ignores how often charges get reduced, dropped, or turn out to be mistaken.

**Participant B (message 4)**

That's a fair point, and I agree bookings don't always reflect what actually happened once a case plays out. Still, I think local police shouldn't be barred from flagging people who pose a real ongoing danger, because federal agents can't act on information they're never given. The better approach, in my view, keeps some cooperation available so serious cases aren't lost in the gap between local custody and federal follow-up. Most of these local policies aren't absolute either, they include some carve-outs for exactly these situations. This is the claim I am relying on. 60% to 70% of sanctuary policy resolutions contain exceptions for violent offenders
