I hear the safety argument, but in my experience the opposite is often true: when immigrant residents fear that any contact with police could lead to deportation, they stop reporting crimes, testifying as witnesses, or even calling for help in emergencies. I'd also gently push back on the idea that this is about picking and choosing which laws matter; local police have distinct duties from federal agents, and blurring that line can undermine trust that took years to build. **Participant A (message 3)**

But I think you can address that without formally restricting cooperation, for instance through community outreach and clear communication policies that reassure victims and witnesses their immigration status won't be used against them in reporting a crime. My worry is that once local departments have blanket policies about what they will or won't share, it takes away judgment in individual cases where cooperation might prevent real harm. Local governments can build trust without asking their police to look the other way when someone poses a danger to public safety.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I agree that individual judgment matters, but broad guidelines are often what actually make individual trust possible, since a person deciding whether to call the police has no way of knowing an officer's private judgment call, only the official policy in place. That's why local governments have been willing to formalize these limits rather than leave it to case-by-case discretion, precisely because clarity is what restores confidence. It isn't about ignoring danger, it's about drawing a boundary so families don't have to guess whether reporting abuse or witnessing a crime will trigger deportation proceedings for a coworker or relative.
