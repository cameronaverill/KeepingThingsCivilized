Public safety should come before political statements, however well-intentioned.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear the consistency argument, but in my experience the opposite happens when local police double as immigration agents: people stop reporting crimes or cooperating with investigations because they fear deportation for themselves or a family member. I think community trust is actually what keeps neighborhoods safer, not blanket cooperation with federal agencies. It's fair to say there are real public safety concerns on both sides, but I'd rather have witnesses willing to talk to police than silence out of fear.

**Participant A (message 3)**

That's a fair point about trust, and I won't pretend fear of deportation doesn't chill some cooperation. But I think the solution is targeted, not a wholesale limitation on cooperation with federal enforcement. Local police can still build trust with communities while assisting federal agencies in clear cases involving serious crimes or repeat offenders. Carving out broad sanctuary policies risks treating all immigration enforcement as illegitimate, when in reality it's the law of the land that police are sworn to uphold in some fashion.

**Participant B (message 4)**

I agree that targeting serious offenders makes sense, and I don't think anyone wants violent criminals protected from consequences. But I think the difficulty is that once local police are entangled with federal enforcement at all, distinguishing cooperation in serious cases from broader dragnet rounded, for immigrants becomes very hard to police. Cities that limit cooperation are often responding to real experiences where good-faith residents were swept up over minor infractions or no infractions at all. People should be able to call for help without wondering if that call could end in a deportation proceeding unrelated to any crime. This is the claim I am relying on.
