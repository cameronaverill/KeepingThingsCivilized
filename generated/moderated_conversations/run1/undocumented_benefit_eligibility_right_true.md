I recognize sanctuary policies come from a place of compassion, but I think local police have more urgent priorities, namely solving crimes and keeping neighborhoods safe. That outcome makes communities less safe overall. Local departments also have limited resources, and I'd rather see those resources devoted to local priorities than acting as an extension of federal immigration enforcement.

**Participant B (message 2)**

Sweeping non-cooperation policies seem to go beyond what's needed to preserve that trust.

**Participant A (message 3)**

I'd push back there, because once police start carving out exceptions like that, the boundary between cooperation and non-cooperation blurs quickly, and that ambiguity is precisely what discourages people from coming forward at all. This isn't solely about compassion for victims, it's about not transforming every police encounter into a potential immigration check, even for someone simply reporting a theft or a dispute at home. The bigger question is what local policing exists to do, and I don't think it exists to serve as a branch of federal enforcement. There's also a fairness angle, since local policing choices have no bearing on how federal benefit eligibility already draws firm boundaries around who can access which programs.

**Participant B (message 4)**

When local police refuse to work with federal immigration authorities, enforcement becomes a patchwork that depends entirely on which city someone happens to be standing in. That inconsistency also signals that certain laws are negotiable, which chips away at public confidence in institutions more broadly. People deserve to know that the law applies the same way regardless of their zip code, even on a subject as contentious as this one, and I don't think that expectation is unreasonable to hold onto.
