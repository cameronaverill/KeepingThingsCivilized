**Participant A (message 1)**

I recognize the safety worries, but in my view local police should limit how much they cooperate with federal immigration enforcement. Their main job is to keep their own community safe, and that depends on everyone trusting them enough to come forward, including people without legal status. When someone is afraid that any contact with police could lead to deportation, they avoid reporting crimes or serving as witnesses, and that makes things worse for citizens and non-citizens alike. Immigration enforcement is a distinct federal responsibility with its own agencies; local officers were never meant to double as immigration agents.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I understand wanting that trust, but I think limiting cooperation with federal authorities undermines the basic rule of law. Police and federal agencies are supposed to work together, and carving out exceptions based on immigration status creates confusion about who is responsible for what. If someone is already in custody for a crime and also happens to be undocumented, I don't see why local law enforcement should refuse to coordinate with federal officials on that separate matter. It feels like local departments are deciding on their own which laws deserve full enforcement and which don't, and that selective approach troubles me.

**Participant A (message 3)**

That's a fair point, and I won't deny that rule-of-law concerns matter to people in a real way. But there's a difference between actively helping hunt down immigration violations and simply declining to treat every undocumented person as a priority for federal cooperation. I think policies limiting cooperation are often narrower than critics assume, usually focused on routine contact like traffic stops, not on shielding anyone genuinely dangerous. The goal is building safer communities through trust, not creating loopholes, and I believe that distinction gets lost in the broader debate.

**Participant B (message 4)**

That distinction is worth noting, and I'll grant that trust-building matters and isn't something to dismiss. Still, I think when local jails decline federal detainer requests even for people already facing separate criminal charges, real gaps open up that let some dangerous individuals slip through entirely. Public safety has to account for everyone, including victims of crimes committed by people who arguably should have been handed over to federal authorities first. I also think debates about whether undocumented immigrants are purely a burden on public resources tend to oversimplify a far more complicated picture. This is the claim I am relying on. Undocumented immigrants pay billions of dollars annually in federal, state, and local taxes
