**Participant A (message 1)**

I understand the worry about dangerous people slipping through, but I think when local police are turned into an extension of federal immigration enforcement, they damage something essential: the willingness of immigrant communities to trust them at all. If a witness or a victim fears that dialing for help might end with a relative being deported, they'll simply stay silent, and that makes every neighborhood less safe, not more secure. I think cities should be free to draw a firm line between local policing and federal immigration matters, so that public safety doesn't get tangled up with immigration status. Blurring that line, in my view, sends the message that some residents can never fully trust the officers meant to protect them.

**Participant B (message 2)**

I hear that concern about trust, but in my view the greater risk is letting local authorities quietly decline to hand over people who are in the country illegally and go on to commit further crimes. I think consistent enforcement of the law matters, and when police departments limit cooperation with federal agents, it can look like certain rules bend depending on who's involved. Communities deserve confidence that legal accountability applies evenly, not that some cases get set aside for political reasons. I do agree that victims and witnesses should never fear coming forward, but I think that goal can be met without a sweeping policy that also shields people from consequences they'd otherwise face under the law.

**Participant A (message 3)**

I take the point about accountability, and I agree that nobody wants someone who's committed a serious crime to avoid consequences because of jurisdictional confusion, but I think there are narrower ways to address that without abandoning limits on cooperation altogether. My worry is that broad cooperation policies don't stop at serious offenses, they end up sweeping in people whose only violation is their immigration status, and that undermines the trust local police depend on every day. I think city governments have an obligation to the residents who live there now, including immigrant families, to make sure policing isn't driven by federal enforcement priorities that have nothing to do with local safety. A policy that protects everyday community trust while still allowing exceptions for serious crimes seems far more sensible to me than folding local police entirely into federal enforcement.

**Participant B (message 4)**

That's a fair distinction, and I agree that policy should be careful not to punish people who pose no real danger, but I think in practice most limits on cooperation end up covering far more than sympathetic cases, and that the underlying question is really about whether immigration status alone should shield someone from standard legal accountability. I think it's worth remembering that the rule of law depends on consistency, and treating unauthorized presence as something local authorities can simply overlook isn't supported by how legal accountability is supposed to work. I think the evidence on how these populations actually behave should inform this debate rather than assumptions on either side. This is the claim I am relying on. Unauthorized immigrants are incarcerated at rates roughly 45% to 50% lower than native-born U.S. citizens.
